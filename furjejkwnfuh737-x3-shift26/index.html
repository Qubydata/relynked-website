Divine,

This is the section every departmental magazine gets wrong. The standard version reads "In January, the department organised an orientation programme for new students." Nobody has ever read that sentence twice.

The fix is counting. "Asked 41 times in the class group in one week. Answered 3 times." Specific numbers are funnier than adjectives, and they're also the thing that makes it feel like a record rather than a joke.

How to build it: one person scrolls the class group from resumption to vacation and writes down every moment that made 20+ people react. That's your timeline. It takes an evening.

14Departmental lore
Pages 14–17 · Departmental lore

Divine,

This is the section I would protect the hardest, because it is the whole reason the magazine is worth keeping.

Every department has stories that everybody knows and nobody has written down. Once the class graduates, those stories die. Lore is you catching them on paper the year they happen.

Two craft rules: write them as stories, not reports (start with a time, a place, a detail), and never print a story that humiliates a named person. "She passed well" is the ending. "The candidate won" is the ending. Nobody is the punchline.

25The Lecturer Dictionary
Pages 25–26 · The Lecturer Dictionary

Divine,

This will be the most-quoted page in the magazine and the one most likely to get you in trouble, so read this twice.

Three rules make it safe: no lecturer is ever named; only phrases submitted independently by three or more students go in; and every academic staff member gets to see the page before it prints, with the option to pull any entry, no questions asked.

That last rule sounds like it will gut the page. It won't. It does the opposite, staff become collaborators, they start submitting their own catchphrases, and the page gets funnier. I have seen this happen. Affection reads completely differently from mockery on a printed page, and staff can tell the difference instantly.

Notice the "AS A NURSE..." entry is not a joke. Put one of those on every humour page. It's what stops the section being cheap.

36Behind the scrubs
Pages 36–38 · The people behind the scrubs

Divine,

Nine interviews, same six questions every year. Same questions is the point, it becomes a format, and in ten years you can line up nine editions and read how the answers changed.

The two questions that do all the work are "What almost made you quit?" and "What would you change in healthcare?" The first makes it human, the second makes it serious. Ask them in that order.

Practical: interview at night, in the place the person actually studies, and record it. Transcribe exactly how they speak. Do not tidy the grammar, "That is my actual answer, not a nice answer" is only good because nobody edited it.

● Clinical nightmares · 31 submissions

THE PATIENT
WHO KNEW
MORE THAN ME

I was explaining diabetes to a man in his sixties. I was doing well. I was using the words. I said "so the insulin helps the sugar move into the cells" and I was proud of that sentence.

He waited for me to finish. He was very kind about it. Then he said: "Which type of insulin is it, my dear? Because I have been taking the long-acting one at night for nineteen years, and the short one before food, and if you are giving me something new I would like to know the onset."

Nineteen years. I had been a nursing student for two.

I said "let me get the nurse in charge" and I walked out of that ward and stood in the corridor and looked at a wall for one full minute.

Then I went back in and asked him to teach me. He did. For forty minutes. He drew the timing on the back of a card. I still have the card.

"That was the day I understood that a patient is not a question I am being asked."Anonymous · 400L · medical ward

Divine,

Most-read page in any student magazine, and the one that can genuinely hurt someone. So it needs a written policy before you open the form, not after.

Mine, and please keep all four: (1) nothing that names or identifies another student; (2) nothing about a patient; (3) a two-person read on every entry, and either can veto without explaining; (4) if a confession suggests someone is in real distress, you tell the counselling unit that submissions like this are coming in, never the submission itself, never a name.

And print the screening note at the bottom of the page like I've done here. It tells 380 students that you can be trusted, which is the only reason anyone submits to edition two.

● Mystery case no.01

THE ANSWER

This is severe pre-eclampsia, and she is showing the signs that come immediately before an eclamptic fit. BP ≥ 160/110 with proteinuria after 20 weeks, plus the warning signs: frontal headache, visual disturbance, brisk reflexes with clonus, and oedema of face and hands.

The thing to notice is that she walked in, apologising. Severe pre-eclampsia does not arrive on a stretcher. It arrives politely.

First four actions, in order

1. Do not send her home or leave her alone. Escalate to the doctor and the nurse in charge immediately.
2. Position her in left lateral, quiet room, low light, cot sides up, suction and oxygen within reach.
3. IV access, bloods (FBC, LFT, U&E, clotting, group), strict fluid balance with an hourly urine output chart.
4. Continuous monitoring: BP every 15 minutes, reflexes, respiratory rate, urine output, fetal heart.

The drug, and what must be at the bedside

Magnesium sulphate for seizure prophylaxis, as prescribed on the unit's regimen. Before the first dose, three things must be at the bedside: calcium gluconate 10% (the antidote), a means of checking reflexes, and a way of counting respiratory rate and measuring hourly urine output. Those three observations are what tell you toxicity is starting: reflexes go first, then respiration, then output.

Antihypertensives lower the pressure. Magnesium is what stops the fit. Those are two different jobs and both are prescribed.

Reviewed by: two clinical instructors, Department of Nursing. Written for revision, not for practice. Always follow your unit's current protocol.

01 What you just flipped through

Not a departmental bulletin. A yearbook, a joke book, a magazine and a time capsule in one object, aimed at one outcome: a student who graduates without a copy feels like they missed part of university.

That one sentence decides everything else. It is why the cover has a name instead of a description, why the HOD's message is inside instead of on page one, and why half the book is made of things students said rather than things the department did.

04 Where the content actually comes from

This is the part people get wrong. You are not writing a magazine. You are collecting one.

One form. Six questions. One QR code on every poster, every noticeboard, every class group, all year. That single form feeds: Only In Nursing, the Lecturer Dictionary, Confessions, Clinical Nightmares, the memes, the photography, the vote, and the time capsule.

The form, exactly

  1. Something you have said out loud that only a nursing student would say.
  2. A thing a lecturer always says, and what it actually means. (No names.)
  3. A confession. Anonymous. Nothing about another student, nothing about a patient.
  4. A clinical moment that humbled you.
  5. A photograph, meme, poem or drawing. Attach it.
  6. Your vote in the Most Likely To categories.

612 submissions from 380 students is the figure printed on page 11 of the mock-up. That is roughly what one form, pushed properly for nine months, gets you. Then editing is selection, not authorship, which is the only version of this job that one student team can actually finish.

05 The team: nine people, not twenty

  • Editor-in-Chief. Owns the deadline and says no. One person, not a committee.
  • Five world editors. One each, as above.
  • Art director. Owns the grid, the two typefaces and the ink colours. The hardest role to replace after the illustrator.
  • Illustrator. Owns the comic and the archetype cards. Pay this person properly.
  • Advertising lead. Owns the rate card and the money. Should not be the editor.

Plus two you borrow: a staff adviser (one lecturer who likes the idea and will open doors) and two clinical instructors who sign off the clinical case. Get the staff adviser in week one. Everything from venue to permission to a subsidy runs through that one relationship.

One more: a Spotify playlist voted for by the class. It costs nothing, it dates the year perfectly, and in 2031 it will hit harder than any photograph.

10 The launch night

This is what turns a magazine into an annual event, and it is the reason the awards exist. One evening, in December, at the end of the session.

  1. The magazine is revealed and handed over. Pre-orders collect their copy here.
  2. The awards are read out and printed certificates are handed over. People keep these for a decade.
  3. The crossword winner is drawn.
  4. The 2027 theme line is announced and submissions open that night.

Nine awards, twenty minutes, one microphone. Do not let it become a ceremony with six speeches. The magazine is the star.

11 The rules that keep you out of trouble

Every one of these exists because a student magazine somewhere learned it the hard way. Write them into the handover document so next year's team inherits them.

  • No lecturer is ever named in a humour section. Phrases only, submitted independently by three or more students.
  • Staff read the Dictionary before it prints, with the right to pull any entry, no explanation needed. This makes them collaborators instead of targets.
  • Nobody is nominated without consent. Anyone can decline, and the category prints as "withheld by request". Print that. It is proof the consent is real.
  • Nothing identifying a patient, ever. "A man in his sixties" is the limit. Every clinical photograph is a staged illustration, captioned as one.
  • Two readers on every confession, either may veto without giving a reason, and the screening policy prints at the bottom of the page.
  • Counselling unit contact on the "I almost gave up" spread, every edition.
  • Paid features marked as paid, on the page, every time.
  • Written consent for every WhatsApp screenshot, from every person in the thread. Redraw them as type. Never paste the real image.
  • Two clinical instructors sign the clinical case before it prints.

"Affection and mockery look identical in a draft and completely different in print. When you cannot tell which one a page is, it is mockery. Cut it."

12 Divine, do these five things this week

  • 01Lock the name and the 2026 theme line. Do not move on until both are decided, because every other choice follows from them.
  • 02Build the six-question submission form and generate its QR code. Put it in the class groups today. The content problem is solved by time, and time starts now.
  • 03Ask one lecturer to be staff adviser. Show them page 68, the clinical case. That page is why they will say yes.
  • 04Find the illustrator. One student who can draw and will commit to six pages. There is always one, and they are the hardest person to replace later.
  • 05Get three written printer quotes: 112pp plus cover, 210 × 275mm, 100gsm text, 250gsm laminated cover, perfect bound, 600 copies. Now you know your real number.

One last thing.

Every idea in this mock-up is worth less than the habit of doing it again. A good one-off magazine is a nice memory. A slightly worse magazine that arrives every single December becomes the thing the department is known for.

So build edition 01 so that edition 02 is easy. Write down the flat plan, the rules, the printer, the rate card and the submission form, and hand all five to somebody in 300 level before you graduate.

Then in 2031, somebody opens page 98, reads their own eighteen-year-old handwriting, and tells everyone it started with you.
